PQuAKE - Post-Quantum Authenticated Key Exchange

Abstract

This document describes Post-Quantum Authenticated Key Exchange (PQuAKE), a proposed construction for mutual authentication using an ephemeral key-encapsulation mechanism (KEM) exchange, encapsulations to certified long-term KEM keys, transcript binding, and explicit key confirmation. Its target use case is a constrained link where avoiding online post-quantum handshake signatures may reduce communication or computation. An optional pre-shared secret is intended to protect encrypted credentials against active outsiders.¶

The goal is to specify a compact reference construction and obtain CFRG review of its security and applicability, with a view to a favorable assessment and publication as an Informational RFC if the review supports it. The document presents the design rationale, assumptions, security intuition, and conditions for safe use. Formal analyses reported for the closely related CAKE-HI construction are relevant evidence; their applicability to this draft's precise messages and algorithms remains to be established. Protocol-specific integration, performance comparisons, and unresolved specification details are identified separately. This individual proposal does not represent CFRG consensus or a recommendation to an IETF working group.¶

1. Introduction

PQuAKE explores mutual authentication without generating a fresh digital signature over each handshake. Each party presents a certificate binding its identity to a long-term KEM public key. Encapsulations to those keys contribute secrets to the final key derivation, and each party confirms possession of the derived key using a message authentication code (MAC). Certificate signatures still require validation; avoiding online handshake signatures does not remove the need for an authenticated trust mechanism.¶

1.1. Problem Statement and Intended Use

The target problem is establishing fresh, mutually authenticated session keys on links where the bandwidth, energy, or implementation cost of post-quantum handshake signatures matters. Candidate applications include constrained devices with provisioned trust anchors and repeated connections to peers with certified KEM keys. The additional KEM ciphertexts, certificate chains, and message flights also have costs. PQuAKE is useful only where those costs are acceptable relative to the alternatives.¶

This is a proposed use case, not evidence that every constrained deployment needs a new AKE construction. Algorithm choice, credential caching, fragmentation, loss, latency, and certificate verification can change the trade-off. A comparison must include both peers' complete transmitted credentials, all ciphertexts and confirmation values, round trips, memory, and measured computation and energy on the target platform. This document does not yet provide a deployment-specific comparison of PQuAKE or claim a universal bandwidth or power advantage.¶

Section 7 and Appendix Table 4 of [CAKE-HI] give the following handshake byte counts for its comparison with mutually authenticated TLS 1.3 using Kyber-1024. The row labels retain the paper's algorithm names.¶

The comparison assumes the paper's message flows and estimated certificates; it is not a measurement of this draft with standardized ML-KEM and ML-DSA encodings.¶

Appendix Table 3 compares cryptographic operation counts: each CAKE-HI peer replaces an online signature generation with an additional static KEM encapsulation and decapsulation. Both designs still validate certificate signatures. This is evidence about the computational workload, not measured device energy or end-to-end latency. Section 8 identifies empirical testing in integrated protocols under bandwidth constraints as future work. The paper's constrained-link use cases and these comparisons support further evaluation, but do not constitute a working-group request for PQuAKE.¶

1.2. Evidence of Protocol Interest

KEM-based authentication addresses an active protocol-design problem. LAKE has adopted [I-D.ietf-lake-authkem-edhoc] as a working-group document for KEM-based authentication in EDHOC. The individual TLS proposal [I-D.celi-wiggers-tls-authkem] specifies authentication with long-term KEM public keys. These efforts establish practical interest in the broader approach and provide protocol-specific alternatives for comparison.¶

There is also an explicit, broader working-group request to CFRG. On September 13, 2026, Renzo Navas wrote on behalf of LAKE, requesting research and specification of lightweight quantum-resistant key-exchange algorithms [LAKE-CFRG-REQUEST]. The request identifies message-size constraints and seeks smaller NIKE and KEM alternatives, with LAKE offering to evaluate candidates. It establishes demand for cryptographic work supporting constrained authenticated key exchange. It does not specifically request review of AuthKEM or PQuAKE, and does not establish that PQuAKE's proposed ML-KEM suite meets the most constrained environments' requirements.¶

The individual IKEv2 proposal [I-D.wang-ipsecme-kem-auth-ikev2] provides more specific evidence for PQuAKE: it explicitly incorporates PQuAKE's ideas, aligns its exchange sequence, and cites PQuAKE for authentication details. This is a concrete proposed application of the construction. An explicit working-group request for CFRG to review PQuAKE, if made, should be cited separately. The protocol efforts above retain their own designs and security analyses; this document seeks review of PQuAKE itself.¶

The absence of a construction-specific request should not, by itself, be interpreted as absence of demand for KEM-based authentication. A working group may draw on participating cryptographers, published analyses, and existing research when developing its protocol, and may direct a CFRG request toward gaps in the available primitives rather than toward review of every integration. It may also seek additional review later as a design matures. These are plausible explanations, not documented motives of LAKE or another working group. LAKE's charter explicitly welcomes formal analysis of cryptographically relevant protocol changes [LAKE-CHARTER]; that does not establish that a proof of its current AuthKEM specification is complete or that the group regards such a proof as replacing CFRG review.¶

Formal proofs and broader cryptographic review serve complementary purposes. A proof establishes specified properties under its model and assumptions; review can assess whether those properties, assumptions, and their mapping to the specification cover the intended use. PQuAKE therefore seeks a shared assessment of a reusable construction and its safe-use conditions, even where an integrating working group has substantial local expertise.¶

1.3. Scope and Publication Objective

The primary objective is to define a compact KEM-based authenticated key-exchange construction and obtain CFRG's assessment of its cryptographic design, security arguments, and conditions for safe use. If review substantiates the claims and the open issues are resolved, the authors seek a favorable assessment of the construction and publication of the reviewed specification as an Informational RFC.¶

The intended result is a reviewed building block for higher-level protocols, accompanied by a stable public reference. It should establish why the construction is suitable for its stated uses and which assumptions and limits an integration must respect. Each integrating protocol still requires its own message mapping, key schedule, and security analysis. Record-layer design and application authorization are outside this scope.¶

The CFRG process [CFRG-PROCESS] asks proposals to establish practical relevance and research support. This revision identifies related constructions and the remaining analysis and interoperability work. The protocol interest described above supports that review objective. A stable reference for external use would also be valuable, but the authors' request includes cryptographic assessment of a construction intended for protocol integration. The appropriate publication stream remains a process question to resolve with the group and chairs. The requested favorable assessment is an objective, not a statement that CFRG has already adopted or approved PQuAKE.¶

1.4. Design Rationale and Related Work

The construction separates three tasks. An ephemeral KEM exchange provides an initial secret and is intended to support forward secrecy. Encryption under a key derived from that secret protects the credentials against passive observers. Two encapsulations to certified static KEM keys add contributions that require the corresponding private keys to recover. Transcript binding and separate confirmation keys tie those contributions to the exchange and each peer's role. The security intuition and its conditions are developed in Section 6.¶

MQV [MQV] and HMQV [HMQV] are historical examples of authentication through key agreement with static and ephemeral keys. PQuAKE borrows that high-level objective, not their algebraic construction or security proofs. Classical discrete-logarithm assumptions do not give post-quantum security. The distinction between possessing a key, authenticating an identity, and confirming a particular session also matters here [BJM-KA].¶

CAKE-HI [CAKE-HI] is the closely related construction for which the authors report CryptoVerif and Verifpal analyses. Its relationship to the specific PQuAKE encoding, key schedule, and acceptance rules must be made explicit before those results support claims about this specification. The distinctive combination proposed for review here is a symmetric exchange of certified KEM credentials, mutual static-key encapsulation, and optional credential hiding against active outsiders using a pre-shared secret. This description is not a claim of novelty or superiority over the related work.¶

1.4.1. Trade-offs of Comparable Constructions

The alternatives below address overlapping KEM-authentication needs, but differ in authentication direction, setup, message flow, and proof model. KEMTLS and KEMTLS-PDK have conference publications; Muckle# has a journal publication. CKAKE and CAKE-HI are public research preprints. Publication of a construction is distinct from standardization of an integrated protocol.¶

  • KEMTLS: [KEMTLS] removes online TLS handshake signatures while retaining signed certificates for long-term KEM keys. It permits the client to send application data after one round trip, under implicit server authentication; explicit authentication requires a later confirmation. The paper identifies weaker forward-secrecy and downgrade guarantees at that early stage than after handshake completion. Its certificate-based client-authentication extension adds a round trip [KEMTLS-PDK]. Thus early data availability, completion of authentication, and mutual authentication have different latency costs. Its TLS-specific key schedule and multistage analysis supply integration detail that a generic construction must provide separately. The Tamarin analyses [KEMTLS-TAMARIN] also show why message flow and security definitions must be compared precisely.¶

  • KEMTLS-PDK: [KEMTLS-PDK] exploits an authentic server public key installed, cached, or obtained out of band. It reduces online bandwidth and computation, and completes client authentication one round trip sooner than ordinary KEMTLS. Large-public-key, small-ciphertext KEMs become more attractive when the public key need not cross the link in each handshake. The operational trade-off is the need to obtain and maintain authentic keys before using this path: provisioning, cache misses, and key updates belong in the deployment comparison. Those operational consequences are inferred from its setup requirement. Public-key pre-distribution is different from PQuAKE's optional symmetric secret, which is used for credential hiding against active outsiders.¶

  • Muckle#: [BATTARBEE-2025-1228] combines conventional, post-quantum, and QKD-derived key material in a hybrid authenticated key-exchange framework, using KEM authentication in place of online signatures. Its journal version reports an additional initiator message relative to TLS 1.3 and makes the initiator the first party able to send encrypted data. Hybrid sources offer a different security objective, with corresponding primitive and key-management requirements; a QKD deployment also needs access to QKD key services. The reported prototype uses simulated QKD, and certificates dominate its measured bandwidth. These conditions prevent directly transferring those measurements to PQuAKE. PQuAKE's three ML-KEM secrets do not establish resilience to failure of ML-KEM itself.¶

  • CKAKE: [CKAKE] provides KEM authentication, forward secrecy, and a composable proof in Constructive Cryptography. Its server-initiated exchange takes one and a half round trips; pre-distributing the server public key permits explicitly authenticated data after one round trip. Its modeled goal authenticates the server to clients rather than establishing PQuAKE's intended mutual credential authentication. The paper's bandwidth comparison counts cryptographic objects and the server public key, excluding certificate metadata and signatures. It reports slightly smaller totals than KEMTLS with ML-KEM, so composability must not be presumed to impose a bandwidth penalty. Its setup resources and guarantees require comparison with an integration's needs. This draft does not claim a composable proof.¶

PQuAKE targets mutual authentication with exchanged certified KEM keys and optional protection of credentials against active outsiders. Its costs include both credential payloads, static-key encapsulations and decapsulations, confirmation traffic, and, for active hiding, provisioning and protection of a symmetric secret. Removing online handshake signatures does not remove certificate signatures or their validation cost. The CAKE-HI analysis [CAKE-HI] is relevant evidence, subject to the model and specification limits in Section 6; it is not an independent alternative demonstrating that PQuAKE outperforms the constructions above.¶

A quantitative comparison must hold the algorithm security level, authentication direction, certificate-chain and caching assumptions, and required authentication and forward-secrecy stage constant. It must count complete encoded messages, fragmentation and transport overhead, and distinguish earliest application data from completed key confirmation. Without such a comparison, this document claims a candidate compact construction for review, not a measured bandwidth, latency, or energy advantage over every alternative.¶

1.5. Security Goals and Component Requirements

The intended goals are session-key secrecy, mutual authentication after key confirmation, fresh keys, replay resistance, and forward secrecy for completed sessions after subsequent compromise of long-term keys. Credential confidentiality against passive observers is a separate goal. Protection against active observers additionally depends on an uncompromised pre-shared secret and the privacy conditions in Section 6.8. These are goals with explicit preconditions, not unconditional guarantees.¶

The components SHALL satisfy the following requirements:¶

  • KEM shared-secret confidentiality under adaptive chosen-ciphertext attack (IND-CCA2), with the selected KEM's input checks and rejection behavior implemented correctly.¶

  • Authenticated encryption for certificates, with confidentiality and ciphertext integrity under the specified nonce discipline.¶

  • A KDF with extraction and expansion properties sufficient for the claimed multi-secret key derivation, with separate domains for credential, confirmation, and application keys.¶

  • A collision-resistant transcript hash and a MAC with the PRF and unforgeability properties required by the analysis. Unforgeability alone is insufficient for the reported identity-hiding argument.¶

  • A certificate signature scheme and validation policy that securely bind the accepted peer identity to the correct static KEM public key.¶

Implicit rejection means that an invalid, correctly sized KEM ciphertext produces a fallback secret rather than a distinguishable decapsulation failure. It does not permit bypassing public-key or input-length checks. IND-CCA2 alone does not establish recipient anonymity or identity hiding. Where a privacy proof uses IK-CCA2 (key indistinguishability under chosen-ciphertext attack), that additional property must be stated and justified for the chosen instantiation; it must not be inferred from ML-KEM standardization. See [NIST-SP-800-227] and [I-D.sfluhrer-cfrg-ml-kem-security-considerations] for KEM use and implementation considerations.¶

1.6. Proposed Version 1 Algorithm Suite

The proposed mandatory-to-implement suite for Version 1 is:¶

  • Certificate encryption: AES-256-GCM with a 96-bit nonce and 128-bit tag.¶

  • Ephemeral and static KEMs: ML-KEM-1024 [FIPS203].¶

  • Transcript hash: SHA-384.¶

  • Confirmation MAC: HMAC-SHA-384, without truncation.¶

  • KDF: HKDF-SHA-384 [HKDF].¶

  • Certificate signature: ML-DSA-87 [FIPS204].¶

Naming these algorithms is not a proof that they instantiate every idealized assumption of a formal model. In particular, the exact HKDF Extract/Expand mapping, labels, output lengths, authenticated encryption inputs, and certificate profile still need to be completed and checked against the models (Section 7.4). This revision is not yet a complete interoperable implementation specification.¶

3. Protocol Description

The PQuAKE protocol consists of four steps. Messages within the certificate, static encapsulation, and confirmation steps may cross in transit. Both messages of a step MUST be available before deriving values that depend on that complete step. A party MUST NOT accept the exchange or release application keys until its peer's confirmation is verified and all credential and authorization checks have succeeded.¶

  1. Establish an ephemeral KEM secret and exchange encrypted certificates.¶

  2. Encapsulate to each peer's certified static KEM key and exchange ciphertexts.¶

  3. Decapsulate and derive separate confirmation and application keys from the ephemeral secret, both static KEM secrets, and the ordered transcript.¶

  4. Exchange and verify key-confirmation MACs.¶

Malformed messages, authentication-tag failures, invalid certificates, failed confirmation, and timeouts MUST prevent acceptance. Avoiding identity-dependent failure signals is an additional privacy requirement; it does not justify accepting invalid credentials or continuing with unvalidated KEM inputs. Error handling and the remaining privacy behavior are discussed in Section 3.6 and Section 6.8.¶

The diagram uses abstract cryptographic operations. KEM.decap(sk, ct) takes the private key first. ct_i, ss_i denote the ciphertext and secret created by the initiator for the responder's static public key pk_r; ct_r, ss_r denote those created by the responder for pk_i. sk_e, pk_e are an independently generated ephemeral key pair for this execution. ss_pre is an optional, high-entropy pre-shared symmetric secret. ss_pre is the empty octet string when no pre-shared secret is used. The logical KDF inputs below follow Figure 1 of [CAKE-HI]; their exact HKDF parameter mapping remains to be specified.¶

H_i and H_r hash the messages sent by the initiator and responder, respectively, in protocol-step order. The transcript T = H_i || H_r always places the initiator's hash first, regardless of which party computes it or the order in which messages arrive. Both parties use the same ordering in the final KDF. Initiator confirmation covers H_i || H_r; responder confirmation covers H_r || H_i, matching Figure 1 of [CAKE-HI]. The hashes are chained: H_i = H(H(H(pk_e) || e_cert_i) || ct_i) and H_r = H(H(H(ct_e) || e_cert_r) || ct_r). The exact message encoding and context binding remain specification work (Section 7.4).¶

The credential-encryption key provides protection before peer authentication. It MUST NOT be used as an authenticated application key. The final key derivation includes ss_e, ss_i, ss_r, and optional ss_pre directly, together with T, as in Figure 1 of [CAKE-HI]. The reported analyses permit adversarial knowledge or choice of ss_pre for properties other than identity hiding. Including it is not a claim of a separately proved PSK-only fallback if all KEM contributions fail.¶

3.2. Exchange certificates

During this step, both nodes establish a shared ephemeral key via a KEM, then use it to encrypt certificates before transmitting them.¶

The initiator generates a fresh ephemeral key pair and transmits the public key. The responder validates that key, encapsulates to it, and returns the ciphertext. The initiator validates the ciphertext's type and length and decapsulates with its ephemeral private key. Both parties derive the credential-encryption key from ss_e and, if present, ss_pre, then encrypt and transmit their certificates.¶

Before using a received static KEM key, each party MUST authenticate its binding to the peer identity using certificate path validation and the deployment's trust and key-usage policy. Validation includes certificate signatures, validity periods, and applicable revocation requirements. An invalid credential MUST NOT lead to successful completion. Identity acceptance and authorization remain separate from signature verification; a valid certificate for an unexpected identity does not authenticate the intended peer. When active identity hiding is required, the decision based on an out-of-band expected identity MUST be deferred until after key confirmation, as in Section 6.3 of [CAKE-HI]. This does not defer checks needed to authenticate and validate the static KEM key before using it.¶

Both directions currently use k_hid. AES-GCM nonce uniqueness MUST hold across both senders using that key. Independently assigning the same nonce in each direction is unsafe. A deterministic allocation of distinct 96-bit nonces by role, or separate encryption keys by role, must be specified and reflected in the formal model. This draft does not yet select that mapping (Section 7.4).¶

3.5. Key Confirmation

Key confirmation uses HMAC-SHA-384 under a separate key for each sender role. The initiator sends C_i <- HMAC(k_C_i, H_i || H_r); the responder sends C_r <- HMAC(k_C_r, H_r || H_i). The responder verifies C_i under k_C_i, and the initiator verifies C_r under k_C_r, using a constant-time comparison and the complete 48-octet MAC value.¶

A party MUST NOT accept a reflected local confirmation as the peer's confirmation. Direction-specific keys and message types distinguish the two messages. Successful local verification shows that the peer can compute the expected confirmation for this transcript, subject to the assumptions in Section 6. It does not show that the peer has received or verified the local confirmation, or authorize application access by itself.¶

3.6. Error Handling

An active attacker can alter, replay, drop, or reorder messages and can cause denial of service. Transcript binding and confirmation are intended to prevent acceptance of an altered exchange, not to guarantee delivery or availability. Implementations MUST validate message framing, type, length, algorithm-specific inputs, and expected protocol step before processing them. Unsupported versions, unexpected message types, truncated fields, and inconsistent lengths MUST NOT lead to acceptance.¶

Timeouts and resource limits SHOULD be configurable and bounded. A failed or abandoned exchange MUST discard its pending key material. A retry MUST start a new exchange with fresh ephemeral keys and encapsulation randomness. Transport retransmission, if supplied by an integration, must be distinguished from advancing protocol state and must not repeat a message in the logical transcript.¶

This draft defines no peer-visible error notification. Such behavior, including any delayed identity-dependent failure, must be specified and analyzed before active identity hiding can be claimed. Logs, response timing, error codes, and differences in subsequent traffic can disclose identities even when certificates are encrypted. Failure handling MUST preserve validation requirements and MUST NOT provide a decapsulation validity oracle.¶

4. Protocol Messages

The message formats below describe the proposed exchange. A checksum is not a cryptographic integrity mechanism; integrity at acceptance depends on authenticated credential encryption and final transcript confirmation. The framing and context still require the refinements identified in Section 7.4.¶

4.1. Message Format

A message of the protocol is formatted as follows:¶

 0               1               2               3 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 0
 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 0 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
 +-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+
|    Version    |     Type      |            Length             |

|                             Data                            ...
+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+-+

Version: 8 bits¶

The version field indicates the format of the initiator hello message. This document describes version 1.¶

Type: 8 bits¶

This field indicates the current step of the protocol.¶

Length: 16 bits¶

Length is the length of the data, measured in octets. This field allows the length of the data to be up to 65535 octets.¶

Data: variable¶

This field changes based on the current step of the protocol.¶

4.2. Hello Messages

The Initiator generates an ephemeral KEM key-pair (sk_e, pk_e) = KEM.keygen() and sends the public key pk_e to its intended recipient (the Responder). The Responder performs encapsulation (ct_e, ss_e) = KEM.encap(pk_e) and sends the ciphertext ct_e to the Initiator.¶

Version: 8 bits¶

The version field indicates the format of the initiator hello message. This document describes version 1.¶

Type: 1¶

This field indicates the state of the protocol.¶

Length: 16 bits¶

Length is the length of the ephemeral public key, measured in octets. This field allows the length of a ephemeral public key to be up to 65535 octets.¶

Ephemeral Public Key: variable¶

The initiator MUST generate a fresh, independent ephemeral key pair for each new protocol execution and MUST NOT reuse the private key across executions. Public keys need not be numerically unique with probability one; freshness requires independent generation with secure randomness.¶

5. Initial Considerations for Protocol Integration

This section identifies questions for future integration specifications. It does not define a mapping of PQuAKE messages into an existing protocol or supply guidance to the corresponding working groups. Each integration must choose its own key schedule and establish that its negotiated algorithms, identities, context, transcript, and acceptance rules preserve the intended security properties. A proof for the standalone construction is not a proof for the resulting protocol.¶

5.1. IKEv2

An IKEv2 [IKEV2] integration would need to define how the exchange relates to IKE SA establishment, identity and AUTH payloads, traffic selectors, and the derivation of IKE and Child SA keys. Negotiation, downgrade protection, fragmentation, retransmission, and failure handling require a dedicated specification. RFC 9370 [RFC9370] concerns multiple key exchanges in IKEv2; it does not by itself define PQuAKE authentication. The individual proposal [I-D.wang-ipsecme-kem-auth-ikev2] provides a concrete example of applying PQuAKE's approach to IKEv2. Its change log states that version 02 incorporated PQuAKE's ideas and version 04 aligned the exchange sequence with PQuAKE; Section 5 refers to PQuAKE for further details of KEM-based authentication. This demonstrates interest in using the construction in a protocol-specific design. Its IKEv2 message formats, key schedule, and security analysis remain the responsibility of that integration specification.¶

5.2. EDHOC

EDHOC [EDHOC] has its own authentication methods, transcript hashes, credential processing, and exporter. Replacing parts of its exchange with PQuAKE cannot be assumed to preserve those properties or its communication costs. Any proposed composition needs a complete message and key-schedule definition and an EDHOC-specific security analysis. PQuAKE's optional pre-shared secret for credential hiding is a choice of this construction, not a requirement or proposal for LAKE.¶

5.3. EAP

A new EAP method would need to meet the EAP key-management framework [EAP], including method identification, peer/server role mapping, exported key definitions, session identifiers, channel binding where applicable, and authentication-server interactions. Fragmentation, retransmission, and deployment authorization also need specification. This draft defines neither an EAP method nor its security properties.¶

6. Security Analysis and Formal Evidence

This section separates the intended security argument from the evidence currently associated with the draft. The authors report computational CryptoVerif and symbolic Verifpal analyses of the closely related CAKE-HI construction [CAKE-HI]. A statement about that modeled construction must not be presented as a proof of every PQuAKE message format, algorithm instantiation, or protocol integration.¶

6.1. Evidence and Model Scope

Sections 5 and 6 of [CAKE-HI] report CryptoVerif and Verifpal results for the construction in its Figure 1. The reported properties and their model-specific meanings are summarized here. The full versioned models, proof scripts, and tool versions remain pending incorporation; the results have not been independently reproduced for this draft.¶

Table 2
Property CryptoVerif Verifpal
Session-key secrecy Real-or-random secrecy Symbolic key confidentiality
Authentication Event correspondence Dishonest-value exclusion at confirmation
Freshness Negligible repeated-key probability No forced successful reuse
Forward secrecy Restricted static-key exposure Phase-limited static-key leakage
Identity hiding Equal-length public-key choice game Credential confidentiality

The CryptoVerif secrecy experiment additionally permits adversarial selection of the initiator's ephemeral key pair. Its freshness experiment compares new keys with stored keys from earlier executions. Identity hiding requires secret ss_pre in both tools. CryptoVerif compares public-key choices by indistinguishability; Verifpal checks confidentiality of credential terms, not an unlinkability query. These distinct definitions must be retained when describing the reported results.¶

For the CryptoVerif forward-secrecy experiments (Section 5.7), an attacker may obtain both static private keys while being unable to modify pk_e and ct_e, or obtain the initiator's static private key while being unable to modify pk_e. Those experiments do not establish unrestricted active forward secrecy under arbitrary ephemeral-message modification and static-key exposure. The Verifpal phase restriction similarly matters.¶

The execution environment in Section 5.2 assumes prior authentic exchange of the parties' long-term public keys. Certificates are modeled as public keys, without metadata or certificate signatures. The CryptoVerif models permit concurrent executions and use a setup table of valid keys. The Verifpal models assume knowledge of valid keys, with a membership check rather than an early expected-identity equality check. These abstractions leave X.509 parsing, path validation, revocation, and application identity policy to the specification and implementation analysis.¶

The two authentication definitions must also be distinguished. CryptoVerif uses event correspondence with the static KEM secrets as parameters. Section 5.12 describes a Verifpal authentication definition that can admit a confirming value generated by the party performing the check. The reported symbolic result therefore must not be restated as independently establishing distinct-peer identity agreement or mutual acceptance.¶

CryptoVerif provides computational evidence under the modeled assumptions. Verifpal provides a complementary symbolic check; Section 5.10 describes its search and soundness limits. Neither tool automatically verifies a wire implementation or an unmodeled error path.¶

6.2. Primitive Assumptions in the Reported Computational Analysis

Section 5.8 of [CAKE-HI] models the final KDF as a random oracle. The credential KDF is modeled as a PRF, except in the forward-secrecy models where it is also a random oracle. Domain separation is assumed. The analysis also requires IND-CCA2 certificate encryption, an IND-CCA2, implicitly rejecting KEM with IK-CCA2 for identity hiding, a PRF for the confirmation MAC, and collision resistance of the transcript hash for freshness. The paper requires the applicable assumptions to hold against the stated quantum adversary with classical oracle access.¶

Section 5.13 describes different symbolic abstractions: HKDF, ENC/DEC, PKE encryption/decryption, MAC/ASSERT, and HASH/CONCAT. These do not validate all details of a concrete ML-KEM, AES-GCM, or X.509 implementation.¶

The exact HKDF-SHA-384 mapping and AES-GCM nonce discipline still need to be checked against the computational assumptions. HMAC's PRF or quantum security does not alone make every concrete HKDF invocation an ideal random oracle. Similarly, a sponge-construction result for SHA-3, cited in Section 5.8 of the paper, does not justify SHA-384, which uses a different construction. Collision resistance of SHA-384 is an explicit assumption here and needs its own supporting rationale.¶

6.3. Relationship to the PQuAKE Specification

This revision aligns the logical diagram with CAKE-HI Figure 1: the optional pre-shared secret contributes to both KDFs, the transcript uses chained hashes of encrypted credentials, and the two confirmation MACs use the corresponding directional transcript order and separate keys. The original draft's inconsistencies in these values cannot be treated as already covered by the reported proofs.¶

A complete mapping still needs the versioned proof artifacts, exact query and acceptance-event definitions, and a comparison of message encoding, HKDF instantiation, nonces, credentials, and error behavior. Published results for CAKE-HI support review of the logical construction; they are not a completed proof of the PQuAKE wire profile or its integrations.¶

6.4. Threat Model and Assumptions

The intended network attacker may observe, initiate, interleave, modify, replay, delay, or suppress exchanges. Credential authenticity depends on trusted certification authorities, correct path validation, and binding the validated identity and key to the intended peer. Corrupted endpoints, compromised trust anchors, implementation side channels, and denial of service are not excluded by network-message cryptographic arguments.¶

A security claim must state which static private keys, ephemeral private keys, encapsulation randomness, pre-shared secrets, and established session keys can be exposed and when. For the forward-secrecy intuition here, the exchange completes between honest parties before static-key exposure, and ephemeral secrets and randomness have been securely erased. No unrestricted claim under concurrent endpoint compromise is made.¶

The confidentiality argument relies on KEM shared-secret security, authenticated encryption, the stated KDF assumptions, and secure randomness. Authentication additionally relies on credential integrity, static-key secrecy in the relevant execution, transcript binding, and unforgeable confirmation MACs. Identity privacy has additional conditions in Section 6.8. An ideal random oracle used in an analysis is not identical to concrete HKDF-SHA-384; that instantiation requires justification.¶

6.5. Session-Key Secrecy and Forward Secrecy: Intuition

The final key depends on ss_e, ss_i, ss_r, and optional ss_pre. In an honest completed execution, an outsider should not recover those KEM contributions from the public keys and ciphertexts. A proof would use the relevant KEM security games to replace protected contributions with random secrets, then justify the secrecy of the derived application key under the KDF assumptions. Merely concatenating secrets is not a complete reduction, and claims for maliciously chosen inputs or exposed contributions need the corresponding multi-session analysis.¶

After later compromise of both static KEM private keys, recorded ct_i and ct_r can reveal the static contributions. The intended protection of the old session key therefore rests on the ephemeral contribution ss_e. Independent ephemeral generation and erasure of sk_e, ss_e, and the encapsulation randomness that could reconstruct it are essential. This explains the forward-secrecy goal for completed sessions; it does not establish security if ephemeral state was retained or exposed, nor forward secrecy of certificates against exposure of their encryption key.¶

6.6. Mutual Authentication and Key Confirmation: Intuition

Consider an attacker impersonating the responder to an honest initiator. The initiator encapsulates ss_i to the static key in the responder's validated certificate. To produce the expected C_r, the attacker needs a confirmation key depending on that secret and the transcript. Without the responder's private key or a forgery, it should not obtain the missing contribution. The corresponding argument for an attacker impersonating the initiator uses ss_r, encapsulated by the honest responder to pk_i.¶

This argument concerns an honest party's challenge to its peer. The encapsulator already knows the secret it creates: success does not imply that both parties necessarily ran a particular decapsulation operation. Relaying an honest exchange also need not reveal its keys to the relay. A full authentication result must establish agreement on peer identities, roles, the transcript, and the session, including concurrent sessions. Certificate validation alone, or equality of derived keys alone, does not establish that agreement.¶

Separate k_C_i and k_C_r prevent simply reflecting the local MAC as peer confirmation. A valid peer MAC confirms possession of a derived key for the local transcript. Each party must also validate the expected peer identity and apply authorization policy before accepting. If confirmation messages cross, one party's local acceptance does not imply the other party has accepted.¶

6.7. Transcript Binding, Freshness, and Replay: Intuition

Both peers derive keys from the same ordered transcript T. Altering a bound message should change the transcript or an underlying secret, so an old confirmation should no longer verify. Fresh ephemeral keys and encapsulation randomness are intended to separate executions. Key freshness is a probabilistic security property, not a guarantee that random outputs never collide.¶

The transcript must have an unambiguous encoding and bind message types, roles, the selected suite, version, and any external application context that changes the meaning of the exchange. Otherwise identical byte strings could be interpreted differently, or a valid exchange could be misbound to a different use. This draft's abstract hashes require the completion identified in Section 7.4 before replay and identity-agreement claims can be tied to an interoperable encoding.¶

6.8. Identity Privacy: Intuition and Limits

Without ss_pre, a passive observer is intended to be unable to derive k_hid from the ephemeral KEM traffic. An active attacker, however, can supply its own ephemeral key or encapsulation and know the resulting ss_e. It can then decrypt a peer's certificate before static-key authentication finishes. Later failure of key confirmation does not undo that disclosure.¶

With an uncompromised, high-entropy ss_pre, the credential-encryption KDF is intended to deny that key to an active outsider even when it controls the ephemeral exchange. A member of the same Community of Interest holding that secret has no such protection. This mechanism is not password authentication: a low-entropy secret can admit offline guessing using the certificate encryption tag and recognizable plaintext. The modeled KDF and encryption assumptions must support the claimed protection for the chosen secret distribution.¶

Credential encryption alone does not establish anonymity or unlinkability of the entire exchange. Static-key ciphertexts, certificate lengths, network addresses, timing, and identity-dependent aborts can distinguish peers. The CryptoVerif privacy model compares equal-length public keys; it does not model arbitrary certificate metadata or traffic analysis. Verifpal's credential-confidentiality query is not the same property.¶

Section 5.8 of [CAKE-HI] invokes the Kyber anonymity result [KYBER-ANONYMITY] to support IK-CCA2. That is relevant evidence, rather than a property inferred from IND-CCA2 alone. Applying the result to standardized ML-KEM-1024 requires checking the definitions, algorithm version, and model instantiation. This draft leaves that mapping open; it makes no unconditional claim of complete identity hiding.¶

Section 6 of [CAKE-HI] reports two instructive privacy failures in weaker variants. Early failure based on an out-of-band expected identity can distinguish peers; the paper delays that decision until the end of the exchange. Hashing plaintext credentials, combined with a MAC assumed only unforgeable rather than pseudorandom, can also disclose identity information. This revision hashes encrypted credentials and retains the PRF assumption for HMAC. Required credential and input validation still must hold; implementation timing and failure paths need separate review.¶

6.9. Limits of the Analysis

Section 5.3 of [CAKE-HI] describes polynomially bounded quantum adversaries with classical oracle access. Section 6.6.2 explicitly leaves superposition access to random oracles outside its completed analysis. A result in that model is not a quantum-random-oracle proof. The relevant post-quantum CryptoVerif methodology is described in [CRYPTOVERIF-PQ]; its applicability depends on the tool version, transformations, and quantum assumptions used in the actual artifacts.¶

A post-quantum KEM alone does not establish those properties for a classical proof or a concrete key schedule. This revision makes neither a QROM claim nor a universal-composability claim. The paper's suggestion that its arguments may extend to QROM is future work, not a proven result.¶

A protocol integration must supply its own composition argument, including application key derivation, negotiation, and acceptance events. Concrete security bounds, multi-user effects, key-compromise impersonation, ephemeral-secret exposure, and stronger identity-agreement properties must be assessed before claiming them for PQuAKE.¶

7. Security Considerations

Safe use requires both the construction-level conditions above and correct handling of credentials, keys, messages, and failures. The following requirements apply to implementations of any completed PQuAKE profile; they do not resolve the specification gaps listed below.¶

7.1. Credential Validation and Authorization

Implementations MUST validate the certificate chain and its binding to the static KEM key, check key usage and algorithm parameters, and enforce the expected peer identity and authorization policy before acceptance. For active identity hiding, the identity-dependent decision is deferred until after confirmation (Section 6.8). Using a valid certificate belonging to an unintended peer is not successful authentication of the intended peer. Certificates containing unsupported or malformed keys MUST NOT be used for encapsulation. A post-quantum certificate signature alone does not make a chain with vulnerable certification keys post-quantum secure.¶

7.2. Key Lifecycle and Randomness

Implementations MUST use cryptographically secure randomness for key generation, encapsulation, and any randomized nonce allocation. They MUST protect static private keys and pre-shared secrets and erase ephemeral private keys, ephemeral secrets, encapsulation randomness, and intermediate KDF material when no longer needed. Failed exchanges MUST discard pending application and confirmation keys. Certificate-encryption and confirmation keys MUST NOT be reused for application traffic. Static KEM keys SHOULD be dedicated to the defined protocol use to avoid unreviewed cross-protocol interactions.¶

A shared Community-of-Interest secret needs provisioning, rotation, revocation, and compromise recovery. Possessing it is not individual peer authentication, and its compromise permits active credential disclosure within the scope described in Section 6.8.¶

7.3. Input Handling and Resource Limits

Implementations MUST validate supported versions and types, exact lengths, field boundaries, cryptographic input formats, and expected protocol state before using an input. A 16-bit length field is not sufficient protection against resource exhaustion. Deployments SHOULD bound concurrent pending exchanges, credential-chain size and depth, fragmentation buffers, and processing time. They MUST NOT release decrypted plaintext on AEAD tag failure or application keys on confirmation failure.¶

KEM checks and implicit rejection MUST follow the selected algorithm's specification. Implementations MUST avoid exposing validity through secret-dependent branches, timing, or distinct decapsulation errors. Generic parser failures still need bounded and safe handling. Side-channel and fault resistance require implementation review beyond the abstract protocol analysis.¶

7.4. Specification Work Required Before Interoperable Use

The following issues require resolution and review before this draft can serve as a complete interoperable specification:¶

  • Define each HKDF Extract and Expand invocation, including salt, input key material, domain labels, context, output lengths, and the no-pre-shared-secret case. Justify the concrete KDF against the formal assumptions.¶

  • Define the exact transcript encoding, including headers, encryption nonces and tags, suite and version, role ordering, and external context. Specify how validated identities and KEM keys are bound to session acceptance and assess unknown-key-share and concurrent-session attacks.¶

  • Allocate AES-GCM nonces safely across both senders under k_hid, or specify separate directional keys, and define authenticated associated data. Include this choice in the formal analysis and test vectors.¶

  • Complete the X.509 KEM-key and signature profile, algorithm identifiers, key-usage rules, chain encoding, and trust and revocation requirements.¶

  • Define privacy-preserving failure behavior without bypassing required checks, and justify any recipient-anonymity assumption for the concrete KEM. Specify credential padding if hiding length is an objective.¶

  • Specify byte order, complete per-type lengths, transport/session association, duplicate and retransmission handling, and extension or negotiation rules. Until negotiation is defined, a completed Version 1 profile must use its fixed suite and reject unsupported alternatives.¶

  • Link versioned proof artifacts for the public CAKE-HI paper and supply claim-to-model and model-to-specification mappings. Provide reproducible test vectors for successful exchanges, wrong peer identities, modified and replayed messages, malformed inputs, mismatched pre-shared secrets, and failed confirmation.¶

These items include cryptographic decisions, not just editorial details. Completing them may require changes to the reported CAKE-HI models. The current diagram and message layouts must not be treated as an already validated wire implementation.¶
